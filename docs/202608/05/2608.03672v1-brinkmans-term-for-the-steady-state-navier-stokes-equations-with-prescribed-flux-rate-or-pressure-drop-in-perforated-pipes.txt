# Brinkman’s term for the steady-state Navier-Stokes equations with prescribed flux rate or pressure drop in perforated pipes 

Richard H¨ ofer ∗ Amina Mecherbet † Gianmarco Sperone ‡

Abstract 

The steady motion of a viscous incompressible fluid in a distorted pipe, containing several small particles of diameter ε3 and mutual distance ε, is modeled through the Navier-Stokes equations with mixed boundary conditions. Apart from inhomogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions on the particles, these involve the Bernoulli pressure and the tangential velocity on the inlet and outlet of the tube, while either the transversal flux rate or the pressure drop is prescribed along the pipe. Applying the energy method in homogenization theory, we study the asymptotic behavior of the solutions to these systems as ε → 0, without any restriction on the magnitude of the data, and show that the effective equations display an additional Brinkman term. An important feature of the present work concerns the required uniform bounds, which are achieved (in the case of the prescribed flux problem) by a contradiction argument based on Bernoulli’s law for solutions of the stationary Euler equations. 

AMS Subject Classification: 76M50, 76D05, 35B27, 35M32, 35Q31. 

Keywords: incompressible fluids, mixed boundary conditions, homogenization, perforated domain.

1arXiv:2608.03672v1 [math.AP] 4 Aug 2026 1 Introduction 

The study of the laminar motion of a viscous incompressible fluid in a junction of pipes Ω ⊂ R3, whose bounding walls are rigid and impermeable, constitutes a fundamental theme in Fluid Mechanics [47] due to its applications in a variety of fields such as aerodynamics [64], hemodynamics [25] and petroleum engineering [9]. In all these disciplines, an essential investigation involves the fluid motion through an array of solid particles or perforations contained in the junction of pipes [7, 39]. More precisely, for an arbitrarily small parameter ε > 0, the fluid flow equations are set in a new domain Ω ε ⊂ R3, obtained by placing inside Ω a large quantity N (ε) of particles, each one of diameter εα (α ≥ 1), at a mutual distance ε from each other. The goal is then to derive an effective description of the system dynamics when ε → 0; mathematically, this can be achieved within the context of homogenization theory [63]. In the seminal works of Marchenko & Khruslov [48, Chapter IV], S´ anchez-Palencia [20, 57], Tartar [62] and Allaire [2, 3], three different regimes have been identified concerning the homogenization of the stationary Stokes or Navier-Stokes equations: 

• In the subcritical regime α > 3, the particles have a vanishing effect as ε → 0, resulting in the original Stokes or Navier-Stokes system in Ω. 

• In the critical regime α = 3, the particles give rise to an additional zero-order term – the Brinkman force – in the homogenized equation. This effective system is governed by the Brinkman equation [11]. 

• In the supercritical regime α < 3, the Brinkman force dominates the viscous forces (and inertial forces in the case of the Navier-Stokes system), resulting in Darcy’s law [17] as the effective limit equation. Since then, homogenization methods in the context of Fluid Mechanics have attracted the interest of several authors. This includes, for instance, the study of convergence rates [31, 40, 58], the evolutionary incompressible and compressible Navier-Stokes equations [23, 33, 36, 50], the study of non-zero boundary data [12, 18, 30, 32], relaxation of the separation assumptions between the particles towards random configurations [12, 27, 28, 30, 34], among various others. Traditionally, the homogenization result for the stationary Navier-Stokes equations is obtained as a by-product of the corresponding result for the Stokes problem via a compact perturbation argument [2, 3, 15]. This approach is unfeasible in fluid flow models where a-priori bounds cannot be derived by simply testing the momentum equation by the solution itself, or else, without imposing a smallness condition on the data of the underlying system. One example is given by the celebrated Leray flux problem for the stationary Navier-Stokes equations in multiply-connected domains, under non-homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions [43]. A similar situation arises in the study of pipe flows with mixed boundary conditions, where the pressure drop ought to be calculated from a given velocity net flux [29, 44]. In fact, the unrestricted solvability of these problems, at the ε-level, is guaranteed by the Leray-Schauder Principle, reaching the required a-priori bounds through a reductio ad absurdum argument that employs Bernoulli’s law [42] for solutions of the stationary Euler equations. Application of homogenization techniques in such models becomes a delicate matter because, if one intends to deploy this contradiction argument for the obtainment of ε-uniform bounds, the complex structure of the perforations must be taken into account. Indeed, this approach was implemented in [54] for the homogenization of Leray’s flux problem in two dimensions, as well as in [59] for the homogenization of the prescribed net flux/prescribed pressure drop problem in a perforated pipe in the subcritical case α > 3. The present paper investigates the homogenization of the steady-state, incompressible Navier-Stokes equations in obstructed pipes in a polydisperse regime with mixed boundary conditions involving the Bernoulli pressure and the tangential velocity on the inlet and outlet of the tube, while either the transversal flux rate or the pressure drop is prescribed along the pipe. Our main contribution is the extension of the results contained in [59] to the critical case α = 3. Moreover, we consider distorted pipes (of finite length) with cylindrical outlets, and we allow non-zero constant Dirichlet boundary conditions at individual particles contained inside the pipes. This feature is relevant for applications, and a first step towards a more complete description that includes the particle evolution. Unfortunately, the rigorous mathematical treatment of the asymptotic behavior of such fully-coupled, dynamical many-particle-fluid problems is extremely challenging and there are only few works in related simplified settings [10, 37, 35]. 2The most significant results of this manuscript, Theorems 2.4 and 2.5, dictate that the homogenization process furnishes Brinkman’s equation in the limit, without any smallness assumption on the data (external force, flux rate or pressure drop). This is achieved by applying the energy method of Tartar [57, Appendix] which, broadly speaking, consists of three steps: (a) Proof of ε-uniform bounds for the solutions of the ε-dependent Navier-Stokes model. (b) Modification (correction) of the test functions in the homogenized domain (without perforations), turning them into admissible test functions at the ε-level. (c) Passage to the limit, as ε → 0, in the Navier-Stokes equations at the ε-level. We emphasize once more that, in most related homogenization results for the (in)-stationary Navier-Stokes equations in perforated domains, step (a) is just a consequence of a simple energy estimate (and a Poincar´ e inequality in the supercritical regime). In contrast, here we exploit the fact that we already anticipate the behavior of the ε-dependent fluid velocity as ε → 0, and therefore incorporate this feature in the aforementioned contradiction argument. This is also in distinction to [59], where the precise way in which the particles influence the fluid flow was irrelevant, since their effect is negligible in the limit as 

ε → 0, in the subcritical case α > 3. More precisely, a scalar capacitary corrector was sufficient in [59] to complete all three steps (a)-(b)-(c), because the relative capacity of the swarm of particles vanishes in the homogenization limit, whereas it just remains uniformly bounded when α = 3, see Lemma 3.1. Therefore, in the current framework, this fundamental role is played by Stokes correctors which, in turn, allow for the construction of a suitable restriction operator in this polydisperse setting. Once step (a) is fulfilled, the same restriction operator allows us to complete phases (b)-(c). Con-trary to the classical procedure due to Allaire [2, Theorem 1.1.8], we pass to the limit by applying the restriction operator to test functions rather than multiplying the momentum equation by an oscillating 

test function. In this regard, here we proceed similarly as in [28], but extending the method to a poly-disperse setting with non-zero particle velocities, and including the convergence of the pressure term in the homogenization limit. Moreover, in contrast to [28, Lemma 2.5], we build a genuine restriction operator, in the sense that it does not modify test functions that already vanish on the particles. Since the discoveries of Brinkman [11], it is well understood nowadays that the spatial distribution of the particles in the critical regime causes the appearance of an additional (friction) force in the fluid equations, originated from the collective reaction of the particles to the drag forces exerted on them by the viscous incompressible flow. Since the critical regime is dilute, the total drag force on the fluid is effectively given by the sum of the individual drag forces of the particles, which are determined by Stokes’ law: they are proportional to the resistance of the particle and the difference between the particle and the fluid velocity in the vicinity of the particle. Mathematically, this phenomenon has been properly identified in the case of zero particle velocities [2, 23], and in [12, 18, 30, 32] for non-zero particle velocities; accordingly, the Brinkman term (2.15)-(2.16) produced by our homogenization process is the sum of a friction term Gu and a source term J that arises from the limiting particle flux. This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we first define the precise geometric setting of the pipes and the perforations, as well as the fluid equations under investigation. We then present the main results of our paper, and the necessary assumptions to reach them. We gather, in Section 3, several preliminary lemmas involving classical tools such as the capacity potential, the divergence equation and the construction of a flux carrier adapted to a perforated domain with non-homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. The Stokes correctors and the restriction operator are also assembled in this section, see Theorem 3.6. Section 4 is devoted to the definition of the functional spaces, the weak formulation of the problems under study, together with the proofs of Theorems 4.3-4.5 concerning the obtainment of ε-uniform bounds. We furnish the proof of our main result, Theorem 2.4, at the end of Section 4. Finally, the Appendix A is devoted to a detailed description of three essential topics which, even though are recurrent in the homogenization literature, are included here for the sake of completeness: the Stokes problem in the exterior of a single particle, the Stokes resistance matrix and the limits of the capacity densities treated in this manuscript. 32 Presentation of the problem and main results
