A SINGULAR PROFILE FOR THE RELATIVISTIC HEAT COST AND THE SPECIAL LAGRANGIAN CURVATURE EQUATION 

XIAOTIAN WU 

Abstract. We study the interior regularity of generalized solutions to the Monge– Amp` ere type equation governing optimal transportation for the relativistic cost c(x, y ) = pa2 − | x − y|2 on Rn. We construct an explicit one-parameter family of radially struc-tured generalized solutions on a ball and exhibit, among them, a solution that is of class 

C1, 12n−1 but of no better H¨ older class: it fails to belong to C1,β for every β > 12n−1 . The construction reduces the equation to a planar autonomous system whose phase variable 

s = ˙ r vanishes to order (2 n − 1) in the base variable. As an application, in dimension two we transfer the construction to the special Lagrangian curvature equation: for ev-ery phase Θ ∈ (0 , π/ 2) we produce a sequence of smooth graphical solutions converging uniformly to a limit of class exactly C1,1/3. Consequently the two-dimensional special Lagrangian curvature equation admits no pure interior C1,β estimate for any β > 13 .

1. Introduction 

Optimal transportation seeks, given two probability measures and a cost function 

c(x, y ) measuring the price of moving a unit of mass from x to y, a map that rearranges the first measure onto the second with least total cost. Since the work of Brenier [2] and McCann, and the regularity theory initiated by Caffarelli [4] for the quadratic cost 

c(x, y ) = |x − y|2/2, a central theme has been to understand how the geometry of the cost dictates the regularity of the optimal map and of its potential. For general costs this is governed by the Ma–Trudinger–Wang curvature tensor and the attendant (MTW) condition introduced in [12]; under (MTW) and suitable convexity of the domains one obtains interior and global regularity (see also Loeper [11], Trudinger–Wang [19], and the monographs [6, 20]), whereas its failure typically permits singular optimal maps. In this paper we study the relativistic heat cost. Fix a positive number a and define

Equation (1.3) is the Monge–Amp` ere type equation expressing that u pushes Lebesgue measure forward to λ times Lebesgue measure under the relativistic optimal map. When the cutoff a is sent to + ∞ it degenerates, after rescaling, to the classical Monge–Amp` ere equation det D2u = λ; the finite value of a is exactly what makes the operator non-uniformly elliptic near |∇ u| = ∞ and is responsible for the singular phenomenon below. Our first result shows that, even for smooth right-hand side, generalized solutions of (1.3) need not be better than C1, 12n−1 , and that this exponent is attained. 

Theorem 1.1. For every n ≥ 2 there is a generalized solution u of (1.3) on a ball 

B ⊂ Rn that belongs to C1, 12n−1 (B) but not to C1,β (B) for any β > 12n−1 .

Here a generalized solution is an Aleksandrov solution, defined through the Monge– Amp` ere measure μu(E) = |Tu(E)| (Definition 2.5). The exponent 12n−1 is sharp and tends to 0 as n → ∞ , so the gradient admits no dimension-free interior modulus of continuity. The solution is an explicit hypersurface of revolution u(x′, t ) = pr(t)2 − | x′|2 whose meridian solves an ordinary differential equation reduced from (1.3), in the spirit of Pogorelov’s singular solutions [14]. It is instructive to compare Theorem 1.1 with the positive regularity theory for opti-mal transportation, since the very same H¨ older exponent 12n−1 marks the sharp threshold there. Assume that the cost satisfies the (MTW) condition and that the two densities are bounded between positive constants (but possibly discontinuous). Loeper [11] proved that the optimal map is then H¨ older continuous, equivalently that the potential belongs to C1,α , with the explicit exponent 14n−1 . Liu [9] subsequently improved this exponent 3

to 12n−1 , which is optimal in the class of merely bounded densities: there exist bounded densities for which the potential is exactly of class C1, 12n−1 and no better, so the exponent cannot be raised without further hypotheses (see [9, 11]). Under the additional assump-tion that the densities are H¨ older continuous or smooth, the (MTW) theory upgrades the potential to C2,α and to C∞ respectively, by the work of Ma–Trudinger–Wang [12], Loeper [11], and Trudinger–Wang [19]. Theorem 1.1 reaches the same critical exponent 12n−1 from the opposite direction. In our example the right-hand side of (1.3) is not merely bounded but constant ; were the relativistic cost to satisfy (MTW), the Loeper–Liu theory just quoted would force the potential to be C∞. The loss of regularity is therefore attributable entirely to the geometry of the cost: the relativistic cost (1.1) violates (MTW) on its whole finite domain 

|x − y| < a ; see the calculation in [17, Section 6]. This failure permits a generalized solution that, despite smooth data, is no smoother than the worst potentials allowed by Liu’s sharp exponent for rough data. Thus the exponent 12n−1 turns out to be critical in two independent regimes: rough data with an (MTW) cost, and smooth data with the relativistic cost. Theorem 1.1 shows that, for the relativistic cost, it cannot be improved by any amount of regularity of the right-hand side. Our second result applies this construction to a geometric equation. Fix Θ ∈ (0 , π/ 2) and consider the two-dimensional special Lagrangian curvature equation with phase Θ, (1.4) arctan κ1 + arctan κ2 = Θ ,

where κ1, κ 2 are the principal curvatures of a surface M ⊂ R3. Introduced by Smith [18] as the curvature analogue of the Harvey–Lawson special Lagrangian equation [7], it admits non-convex graphical solutions, and its interior regularity is delicate. Related interior curvature estimates for three-dimensional hypersurfaces of prescribed scalar cur-vature were established by Qiu [15]. For the special Lagrangian curvature equation, Qiu and Zhou [17] proved a priori interior estimates under structural hypotheses, and their Lemma 6.1 (our Lemma 4.1) links (1.4), for a suitable choice of parameters, to the relativistic equation (1.3). Exploiting this link together with Theorem 1.1, we realize the singular profile as a limit of smooth solutions, in the spirit of the singular special Lagrangian solutions of Wang–Yuan [21]. 

Theorem 1.2. On a ball B ⊂ R2 there exist smooth graphical solutions uj (j ≥ 1) of

regularity at the center. The exponent 1 /3 is the case n = 2 of Theorem 1.1, here attained as a uniform limit of smooth solutions. This should be contrasted with the two-dimensional Monge–Amp` ere equation. For convex solutions of det D2u = f > 0 in the plane, pure interior second-derivative esti-mates are available: the classical estimate is due to Heinz [8], and more recent proofs were given by Chen–Han–Ou [5] and Liu [10]. In those results the interior Hessian is controlled in terms of the C0 size of the solution and the prescribed data, with no sepa-rate gradient bound. Theorem 1.2 shows that this feature does not persist for the special Lagrangian curvature equation: even for smooth graphical solutions with fixed phase, uniform C0 control does not prevent the loss of gradient H¨ older regularity. The proof of Theorem 1.1 proceeds in three steps. First a formal calculation shows that the radial ansatz u(x′, t ) = pr(t)2 − | x′|2 reduces (1.3) to the scalar second order ODE (3.5) for the meridian r. Imposing r(0) = a, ˙r(0) = 0 forces the radial factor 

A = 1 − a/ (r√1 + ˙ r2) to vanish at t = 0, which in turn forces ¨ r to blow up; tracking the leading asymptotics A ∼ s2/2 with s = ˙ r yields ˙ s ∼ s−2( n−1) and hence |s| ∼ | t|1/(2 n−1) ,the source of the exponent. Second, to make this rigorous through the degeneracy at t = 0 we recast the dynamics as a first order autonomous system in the phase variable s, equations (3.7), whose right-hand side is smooth at s = 0; the Picard– Lindel¨ of theorem then produces a smooth solution, and the inversion t 7 → s(t) supplies the sharp asymptotics in (3.9). Third, we verify that the resulting u is an Aleksandrov solution (Lemma 3.7): c-convexity is obtained from a one-dimensional support inequality for r promoted to the full graph by a reverse triangle inequality for the relativistic cost (Lemma 2.6), and the Monge–Amp` ere measure is computed exactly by slicing the image of Tu. Finally, the sharp C1, 12n−1 regularity of u is checked in Lemma 3.8. Theorem 1.2 is then obtained by a perturbation argument: the autonomous system is perturbed at the initial value, as in (4.1), so that each perturbed solution is everywhere 

smooth and, via Lemma 4.1, gives a smooth graphical solution of (1.4) for the param-eters a = tan Θ, λ = 1 / cos 2 Θ; continuous dependence on initial data then forces the perturbed solutions to converge in C0 to the singular profile of Theorem 1.1 with n = 2. This section fixes the notions from the theory of optimal transportation that we use, specialized to the relativistic cost (1.1), and proves the one elementary inequality used in the verification of c-convexity in Section 3. For background on c-convexity, optimal maps, and the Aleksandrov formulation of the associated Monge–Amp` ere equation, see [6, 20]. Throughout, Ω ⊂ Rn is a domain and c is the cost (1.1). 2.1. c-convexity and the c-normal map. The role played by affine functions in clas-sical convexity is played here by the translates of the cost. 

Definition 2.1. A function ℓ is c-linear if ℓ(·) = c(·, y ) + b for some y ∈ Rn and b ∈ R.A c-linear function is thus a vertical translate of a single sheet c(·, y ) of the cost, which for the relativistic cost (1.1) is a lower hemisphere of radius a centered at ( y, 0). Replacing supporting hyperplanes by supporting c-linear functions yields the notion of 

c-convexity. 

Definition 2.2. A lower semi-continuous function ϕ on Ω is c-convex in Ω if for every 

x0 ∈ Ω there is a c-linear function ℓ that supports ϕ from below at x0, that is, ℓ ≤ ϕ in Ω and ℓ(x0) = ϕ(x0). Such an ℓ is called a c-support of ϕ at x0.The collection of points y that generate supporting c-linear functions at a given x0 is the analogue of the subdifferential of a convex function; recording it defines the c-normal map, which plays the role of the gradient map. 

Definition 2.3 (c-normal map) . Let ϕ be c-convex on Ω. Its c-normal map is the set-valued map Tϕ : Ω ⇒ Rn assigning to x0 ∈ Ω the set of all y0 ∈ Rn for which 

c(·, y 0) + b is a c-support of ϕ at x0 for some b ∈ R.

Remark 2.4. If a c-convex function ϕ is C1, then Tϕ is single-valued and is deter-mined by the first-order matching condition ∇xc(x, T ϕ(x)) = ∇ϕ(x). For the relativistic cost (1.1) this is exactly the explicit formula (1.2). 2.2. Generalized solutions. 2.3. A reverse triangle inequality for the relativistic cost. The verification that our profile is c-convex reduces a multidimensional support inequality to a one-dimensional one by means of the following superadditivity property of the map ( p, v ) 7 → pp2 − | v|2

on the forward cone {p ≥ | v|} . Geometrically it is the reverse triangle inequality of Lorentzian geometry, and it is exactly what makes the relativistic cost compatible with the radial construction. 

Lemma 2.6. Let p, q > 0 and v, w ∈ Rn−1 satisfy p ≥ | v| and q ≥ | w|. Then 

(2.2) p(p + q)2 − | v + w|2 ≥ pp2 − | v|2 + pq2 − | w|2.

Proof. Both sides of (2.2) are non-negative, so squaring shows that (2.2) is equivalent to 

pq − v · w ≥ pp2 − | v|2 pq2 − | w|2.

Since v · w ≤ | v| | w| by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, it suffices to prove the stronger inequality 

pq − | v| | w| ≥ pp2 − | v|2 pq2 − | w|2.

Here the left-hand side is non-negative because pq ≥ | v| | w| by hypothesis, so we may square once more; the resulting inequality simplifies to ( p |w| − q |v|)2 ≥ 0, which holds. This proves (2.2). □

Remark 4.3. We present a geometric perspective through the parallel surface transform. For the relativistic cost (1.1), a c-support is the lower cap of a sphere of radius a tangent from below to the graph M = {(z, u (z)) } ⊂ R3. Thus Tu(z) is the horizontal projection of the center of that sphere, or equivalently the projection of the parallel surface 

Ma := {p + a ν (p) : p ∈ M} , a = tan Θ ,

where ν is the unit normal oriented toward the supporting spheres. With λ = 1 / cos 2 Θ, equation (1.3) says that Ma has constant Gaussian curvature, while Lemma 4.1 rewrites the same condition as (1.4) on M. In our sequence the offsets are rotationally symmetric constant-Gauss-curvature surfaces. The limit degenerates when r(0) = a, so the parallel surface collapses onto the axis at t = 0.
