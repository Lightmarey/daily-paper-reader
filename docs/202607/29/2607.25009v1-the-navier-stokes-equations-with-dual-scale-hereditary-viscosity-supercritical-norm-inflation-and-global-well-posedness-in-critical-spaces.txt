While the classical model is a triumph of continuum mechanics, the increasing need to understand complex fluid behaviors has driven the community to investigate non-local and anomalous momentum transport. (1.1) The introduction of the memory kernel g ∈ L1loc (R+) is not merely a mathematical abstrac-tion, but a phenomenological necessity in the rational mechanics of non-Newtonian fluids and viscoelastic turbulence (see Barbu and Sritharan [3]). In highly heterogeneous fluids—such as polymer melts, micellar networks, and concentrated suspensions—momentum transport exhibits a fading memory effect. In this work, we assume that g is a dual-scale admissible kernel. Much of the classical literature on fractional viscoelasticity employs single-scale power-law kernels (equivalent to standard Caputo or Riemann-Liouville fractional derivatives). However, as extensively docu-mented in modern rheology (e.g., Jaishankar and McKinley [17] and Mainardi [21]), a single power-law implies an infinite zero-shear viscosity and fails to capture the terminal relaxation of some real complex fluids. The dual-scale kernel resolves this physical paradox: its Laplace transform encodes an anomalous, highly elastic response at short times (high frequencies) governed by an exponent α∞ ∈ (0 , 1), which continuously crosses over to a distinct fluid-like terminal relaxation at long times (low frequencies) characterized by α0 ∈ [0 , 1). From a mathematical perspective, this physical fidelity comes at a severe analytical cost: the continuous crossover between distinct asymptotic regimes permanently breaks the exact global scale invariance of the system. 1.2. State of the art and mathematical challenges. A central feature of the classical Navier-Stokes model is its exact scale invariance, which dictates the topological boundaries for local and global existence. This trajectory was rigorously in-augurated by Kato [18], who established the well-posedness of strong solutions in the critical Lebesgue space LN (RN ). Subsequent advancements led to seminal results in critical Besov spaces by Cannone [6], culminating in the optimal well-posedness threshold in BM O −1 by Koch and Tataru [19]. Determining whether fractional and integro-differential variants of fluid models inherit this catastrophic collapse requires a robust mild framework. The inherent absence of a global self-similar scaling dictates that the problem must be tackled via a highly refined pseudo-differential harmonic analysis. Example 1 (Pure fractional viscoelasticity). The standard power-law fluid model, extensively validated in modern rheology for complex bulk and interfacial dynamics [17], is recovered by choosing the Riemann-Liouville fractional kernel, which yields the Laplace transform ˆ g(λ) = λ−α for α ∈ (0 , 1). Example 2 (Multi-term viscoelastic relaxation). In complex viscoelastic fluids pre-senting structural heterogeneities (e.g., polymer melts), the momentum transport mecha-nism often transitions between distinct anomalous regimes over time. These dynamics can be modeled by a sum of fractional kernels, an approach systematically detailed in the theory of linear viscoelasticity [21], yielding ˆ g(λ) = c1λ−α + c2λ−β with 0 < β < α < 1. Example 3 (Fractional retardation and Cole-Cole relaxation).
